The Lighthouse Keeper

by Anne Wanderly


CHAPTER I

The Letter


The letter came on a Tuesday, which was the day the supply boat came,
and so it was the only day anything ever came to the island of Saint
Brannoc. Elias Marrow turned it over twice in his hands before he opened
it. The paper was thick and cream-coloured, and the handwriting on the
front was small and very straight, like a row of soldiers who had been
told to stand still.

"You'll want to sit down for that one," said Tobin, the boatman, who
could not read but could always tell what a letter meant by the way it
was folded. "That's a solicitor's fold, that is. Nothing good comes
folded like that."

Elias did not sit down. He had not sat down in the middle of the day for
eleven years, not since he first climbed the hundred and forty-two steps
of the Brannoc Light and decided that a keeper who sat down was a keeper
who fell asleep. He read the letter standing on the jetty, with the wind
pulling at the corners of it, and when he had finished he folded it
again along the solicitor's fold and put it in the inside pocket of his
coat.

"Well?" said Tobin.

"They are putting in an electric lamp," said Elias. "In the spring. It
will turn itself on at dusk and off at dawn, and it will not need a
keeper."

Tobin whistled, long and low, and for a while neither of them said
anything at all. The gulls said a great deal, as gulls do, but none of
it was helpful.


CHAPTER II

The Long Winter


That winter was the hardest anyone on the coast could remember. The sea
froze in the little harbour at Penhallow, which it had not done since
the old king's time, and the wind came down from the north with ice in
its teeth. Every night Elias climbed the hundred and forty-two steps,
trimmed the wick, polished the great glass lens until it shone like a
second moon, and wound the clockwork that turned it.

He kept a logbook, as keepers must. For eleven years the entries had
been short: the weather, the hours of the lamp, the ships that passed.
But that winter the entries began to grow longer. He wrote down the
colour of the sky at the moment the lamp was lit, which was never the
same colour twice. He wrote down the names of the ships and guessed at
the names of their captains. He wrote down the shape of the seals on the
north rocks, and the night the aurora came, green and trembling, and
hung over the island for an hour like a curtain no one dared to open.

"You are writing a book," said Tobin in February, when the boat could
finally get through the ice and he saw the logbook lying open on the
table.

"I am keeping a record," said Elias.

"It's a book," said Tobin firmly. "I can't read it, but I know a book
when I see one. It's got that look about it."

* * *

On the last night of February a ship came out of the storm without any
lights at all. Elias saw her only because the beam swung across her
sails at the very moment the snow thinned, and he saw that she was too
close, much too close, and running straight for the Teeth, the black
rocks that had given the island its bad name long before there was ever
a light on it.

There is nothing a keeper can do in such a moment except keep the light.
Elias kept it. He stood beside the lens with his hand on the clockwork
and he did not move, and the beam swung round, and round, and round
again, and on the third turn the ship's bow began, very slowly, to come
about.


CHAPTER III

Spring


The engineers arrived in April with their crates and their cables and
their cheerful, careless voices. They were kind to Elias, in the way
that people are kind to things they are about to replace. They showed
him the new lamp, which was small and bright and humming, and they
explained that it would never need trimming, never need polishing, never
need winding, never need anything at all.

"It's a marvel," said the youngest engineer. "It will never get tired."

"No," said Elias. "I don't suppose it will."

On his last morning he carried his trunk down the hundred and forty-two
steps, and the logbook was on top of everything else in the trunk,
wrapped in oilcloth against the spray. Tobin was waiting at the jetty
with the boat.

"Where will you go?" Tobin asked.

Elias looked back at the tower, white against the soft spring sky. The
new lamp was dark, waiting for dusk, and the windows of the lantern room
shone with the morning sun as if someone up there were still polishing
them.

"To Penhallow, I think," he said. "There is a printer there who owes me
a favour. I have a book that needs setting in type."

And so it was that the record of the Brannoc Light, eleven years and one
long winter of it, came to be printed on good paper and bound in blue
cloth, with the shape of a lighthouse pressed in gold upon the cover.
You may still find a copy, if you look in the right shop on the right
kind of afternoon. It has that look about it.

THE END
